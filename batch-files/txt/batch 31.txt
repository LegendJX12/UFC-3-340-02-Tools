CHAPTER 6 SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN EXPLOSIVE FACILITY DESIGN
INTRODUCTION
6-1 PURPOSE.
The purpose of this manual is to present methods of design for protective construction 
used in facilities for development, testing, production, storage, maintenance, 
modification, inspection, demilitarization, and disposal of explosive materials.
6-2 OBJECTIVE.
The primary objectives are to establish design procedures and construction techniques 
whereby propagation of explosion (from one structure or part of a structure to another) 
or mass detonation can be prevented and to provide protection for personnel and 
valuable equipment.
The secondary objectives are to:
(1) Establish the blast load parameters required for design of protective 
structures.
(2) Provide methods for calculating the dynamic response of structural elements 
including reinforced concrete, and structural steel.
(3) Establish construction details and procedures necessary to afford the 
required strength to resist the applied blast loads.
(4) Establish guidelines for siting explosive facilities to obtain maximum cost 
effectiveness in both the planning and structural arrangements, providing 
closures, and preventing damage to interior portions of structures because of 
structural motion, shock, and fragment perforation.
6-3 BACKGROUND.
For the first 60 years of the 20th century, criteria and methods based upon results of 
catastrophic events were used for the design of explosive facilities. The criteria and 
methods did not include a detailed or reliable quantitative basis for assessing the 
degree of protection afforded by the protective facility. In the late 1960's quantitative 
procedures were set forth in the first edition of the present manual, “Structures to Resist 
the Effects of Accidental Explosions.” This manual was based on extensive research 
and development programs which permitted a more reliable approach to current and 
future design requirements. Since the original publication of this manual, more 
extensive testing and development programs have taken place. This additional research 
included work with materials other than reinforced concrete which was the principal 
construction material referenced in the initial version of the manual.

Modern methods for the manufacture and storage of explosive materials, which include 
many exotic chemicals, fuels, and propellants, require less space for a given quantity of 
explosive material than was previously needed. Such concentration of explosives 
increases the possibility of the propagation of accidental explosions (one accidental 
explosion causing the detonation of other explosive materials). It is evident that a 
requirement for more accurate design techniques is essential. This manual describes 
rational design methods to provide the required structural protection.
These design methods account for the close-in effects of a detonation including the high 
pressures and the nonuniformity of blast loading on protective structures or barriers. 
These methods also account for intermediate and far-range effects for the design of 
structures located away from the explosion. The dynamic response of structures, 
constructed of various materials, or combination of materials, can be calculated, and 
details are given to provide the strength and ductility required by the design. The design 
approach is directed primarily toward protective structures subjected to the effects of a 
high explosive detonation. However, this approach is general, and it is applicable to the 
design of other explosive environments as well as other explosive materials as 
mentioned above.
The design techniques set forth in this manual are based upon the results of numerous 
full- and small-scale structural response and explosive effects tests of various materials 
conducted in conjunction with the development of this manual and/or related projects.
6-4 SCOPE.
It is not the intent of this manual to establish safety criteria. Applicable documents 
should be consulted for this purpose. Response predictions for personnel and 
equipment are included for information.
In this manual an effort is made to cover the more probable design situations. However, 
sufficient general information on protective design techniques has been included in 
order that application of the basic theory can be made to situations other than those 
which were fully considered.
This manual is applicable to the design of protective structures subjected to the effects 
associated with high explosive detonations. For these design situations, the manual will 
apply for explosive quantities less than 25,000 pounds for close-in effects. However, 
this manual is also applicable to other situations such as far- or intermediate-range 
effects. For these latter cases the design procedures were originally developed for 
explosive quantities in the order of 500,000 pounds which is the maximum quantity of 
high explosive approved for aboveground storage facilities in the Department of 
Defense manual, “DoD Ammunition and Explosives Safety Standards,” DOD 6055.09-
M. \1\ However, DOD 6055.09-M currently addresses explosive quantities of up to 
5,000,000 lbs, and the design procedures in this document have been extrapolated for 
designs to resist accidental explosions on ships of 5,000,000 lbs or more. Although 
some disasters have involved very high explosive quantities (such as Port Chicago in 
1944 which involved around 10,000,000 pounds of explosives), limited testing has been

6-5 FORMAT.
This manual is subdivided into six specific chapters dealing with various aspects of 
design. The titles of these chapters are as follows:
Chapter 1 Introduction
Chapter 2 Blast, Fragment, and Shock Loads
Chapter 3 Principles of Dynamic Analysis
Chapter 4 Reinforced Concrete Design
Chapter 5 Structural Steel Design
Chapter 6 Special Considerations in Explosive Facility Design
When applicable, illustrative examples are included in the Appendices.
Commonly accepted symbols are used as much as possible. However, protective 
design involves many different scientific and engineering fields, and, therefore, no 
attempt is made to standardize completely all the symbols used. Each symbol is defined 
where it is first used, and in the list of symbols at the end of each chapter.
6-6 CHAPTER CONTENTS.
This chapter contains procedures for the design of blast resistant structures other than 
above ground, cast-in-place concrete or structural steel structures, as well as the design 
of other miscellaneous blast resistant components. Included herein is the design of 
reinforced and non-reinforced masonry walls, precast elements with both prestressed 
and conventional reinforcement, pre-engineered buildings, suppressive shielding, blast
resistant windows, underground structures, earth-covered arch-type magazines, blast 
valves and shock isolation systems. 
\1\
MASONRY
6-7 GENERAL INFORMATION.
Masonry walls can provide economical resistance to relatively low external blast 
overpressures when properly designed and detailed, along with other advantageous 
characteristics such as limited fragment protection (solid masonry), fire protection, 
acoustical and thermal insulation, and modular construction. General requirements and 
limitations on the use of masonry for blast resistant design are discussed in this section. 
Sections 6-8 and 6-9 discuss specific requirements related to blast design with

6-7.1 Limits on the Use of Masonry Construction.
New masonry construction in this UFC is limited to one-story, reinforced CMU walls 
placed in a running bond with single or multiple wythes. CMU walls may be designed to 
satisfy Protection Category 1 and Protection Category 2 requirements. All new CMU 
walls shall be reinforced with vertical steel reinforcing bars that are designed in 
accordance with Section 6.8. The information in Section 6.9 can be used to determine if 
existing unreinforced masonry walls provide a sufficient level of protection. 
Non-solid masonry walls with any ungrouted voids, including reinforced masonry walls 
that are not fully grouted, may only be used to provide protection against far-range, 
external blast loading (i.e. only at charge scaled standoffs greater than 3 ft/lb1/3) where 
there is no direct primary or secondary fragmentation threat. This requirement 
effectively limits use of these walls to building sides that do not face towards the 
potential explosion site. Solid, or fully grouted reinforced masonry walls can be 
designed to provide required fragment protection as described in Section 6-7.3.7. They 
may also be used at close-in scaled standoffs (i.e. at charge scaled standoff distances 
of 3 ft/lb1/3 and less) if they can be designed to satisfy the required Protection Category 
requirements and spall protection as described in Section 6-7.3.6. 
The design of new masonry walls to resist internal blast loads is not recommended as 
there are very limited data for this application. For applications involving existing 
masonry, the wall may not be subjected to combined flexure and tension under internal 
or external blast loading. Masonry walls may not be used as substantial dividing walls.
Masonry walls are designed to resist blast loads in flexure. Per Section 6-7.4, tension 
membrane action and compression membrane action may not be considered. For 
existing unreinforced masonry walls, the additional resistance from axial load arching 
may be incorporated into the resistance-deflection curve in accordance with Section 6-
9.2.

Masonry materials consist of masonry units and mortar, which is used to connect the 
units into a structural component. They can also include reinforcing steel and grout, 
which causes the reinforcing steel to be composite with the masonry, as well as 
secondary wire reinforcement placed in the horizontal bed joints between courses of

6-7.2.1 Masonry, Mortar, and Grout Materials.
Figure 6-1 (+) shows typical shapes for different types of masonry units. Brick and CMU 
are the most commonly used masonry units in the United States (U.S.) and Canada, 
with some use of clay tile. Generally, a masonry unit is described based on its type and 
its nominal thickness dimension. The actual dimensions of manufactured masonry units 
are 3/8 inch less than nominal dimensions to allow for the mortar thickness and the 
thickness of typical interior wall material (i.e. gypsum board). All masonry units must 
conform to applicable ASTM requirements in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 (e.g. CMU 
must conform to the requirements of ASTM C90). As shown in Figure 6-1, there are 
many variations of CMU block shapes, including the conventional “stretcher” block, 
bond beam units, and “A” and “H” shaped blocks that are placed around vertical 
reinforcement rather than lifted over the reinforcement. All commonly used CMU 
configurations that comply with ASTM C90 are acceptable for blast design.
Mortar should conform to the requirements of ASTM 270. There are several types of 
mortar (i.e. Type M, Type S, and Type N) that are commonly used to attach the 
masonry units together. There are no available data indicating that any of these mortar 
types are more effective against blast loads. Therefore, all mortar types are acceptable 
for blast resistant walls. Grout, which is very similar to concrete without large aggregate,
is used in the cells of CMU and in the void space between brick and CMU wythes to 
bond reinforcing bars to the masonry. The grout should comply with the requirements of 
ASTM 476 and have sufficient plasticity and vibration during placement to avoid air 
voids or honeycombing unless self-consolidating grout is used. Construction of blast 
resistant masonry walls should comply with Level C Quality Assurance according to 
Chapter 1 of TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5. Although only the reinforced cells in CMU are 
typically grouted in conventional design, all cells are commonly grouted for explosive 
safety design.
The manufacturing of CMU depends in part on locally available materials, but most 
modern CMU construction is with lightweight CMU. Heavier weight CMU has been used 
more in older construction and cold weather climates. It is usually conservative to 
assume lightweight CMU (i.e. a unit weight of approximately 95 lb/ft3
) for blast design or 
analysis because this will result in the least calculated mass and corresponding inertial 
resisting force.

6-7.3.2 Masonry Compression Strength.
The compression strength of the masonry units, grout, and mortar acting as a unit are 
measured together in a masonry prism, which consists of two masonry units with a 
mortared joint that is tested in compression. The cells of the prism are grouted if the wall 
is grouted. If the wall construction is partially grouted (contains both ungrouted and 
grouted cells), then grouted and ungrouted prisms are tested and the compression 
strength is averaged. The prism is constructed in the field using the materials and 
workmanship at the jobsite and then tested in the laboratory in a manner similar to 
concrete cylinders. The resulting compression strength (f'm) is used in design similarly to 
the concrete cylinder strength (f'c). For new blast resistant design, a minimum strength 
of 2000 psi is required for f’m. Additionally, a minimum grout strength of 3000 psi is 
required to enhance composite action between the reinforcing steel and masonry. The 
modulus of elasticity of a masonry component can be calculated as shown in Equation

6-7.3.3 Masonry Flexural Design.
The flexural strength of masonry for blast design is based on a dynamic flexural tensile 
strength (i.e. modulus of rupture), fdt, of 200 psi. If f’m exceeds 2000 psi, fdt can be taken 
as 10% of f’m provided that it does not exceed 250 psi. These values represent the 
dynamic adhesion strength between the mortar and masonry along a bed joint at strain￾rates representative of masonry response to far-range blast loading. Masonry flexural 
strength is only used in the analysis of unreinforced masonry walls since the flexural 
strength of reinforced masonry walls is based on the tensile strength of the reinforcing 
steel, as discussed in Section 6-8. 
The values for fdt were developed by a trial and error procedure where they caused 
single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) analyses to match the measured response of 
unreinforced masonry walls well, on the average, from a large number of high explosive 
tests (i.e. over 50 tests), where wall response was observed primarily in terms of 
damage levels and a limited number of measured deflections (Oswald, 2005). These 
values for fdt are applicable for modern masonry construction (i.e. since 1960) that is in 
good condition, laid in running-bond pattern, and without substantial infilling of former

6-7.3.7 Penetration Resistance.
Only solid masonry walls should be used for walls subject to fragment impact from an 
accidental explosion. Fragment penetration through solid masonry should be 
determined with the same approach as penetration through concrete in Section 4-60.4 
using the masonry material compression strength, f’mc, in place of the concrete 
compression strength, f’c. The value of f’mc should be the lesser of the masonry block or 
grout compression strength. Conservatively, f’mc can be assumed equal to 1900 psi for 
masonry that is in good condition unless more accurate information is available. 
Calculations using the procedures outlined in this section (i.e. using concrete 
penetration equations in Section 4-60 and values for f’mc in place of f’c) overpredicted all 
measured penetration depths and matched all perforation cases for experiments where 
twenty-five small steel cubes (i.e. 0.5 inch and 0.63 inch) were shot into unreinforced 
CMU, grouted CMU, and brick walls at velocities between 3000 ft/sec and 5000 ft/sec 
(Bowles, et al 1998).

6-7.4 Masonry Wall Construction.
6-7.4.1 Multi-Wythe Walls.
A masonry wall can be constructed as a single, monolithic wythe with one or more units 
through the thickness acting compositely, or as a cavity wall consisting of multiple, 
closely spaced masonry wythes with an air gap or insulation between wythes. Many 
modern masonry walls in the northern part of the U.S. and Canada are cavity walls to 
provide required insulation and help prevent moisture migration into the building. Figure 
6-3 (+) shows a cavity wall constructed with brick and CMU. Cavity walls can also be 
constructed with other types of masonry. These walls are connected together across the 
gap between wythes with ties or tabs, as illustrated in Figure 6-3, which cause both 
wythes to deflect together in a non-composite manner when conventional lateral loads 
are applied to the outer wythe. The outer wythe of multi-wythe walls may be referred to 
as a veneer wall, which is typically an unreinforced, non-structural wall that acts only as 
a facade and prevents moisture penetration.
New blast-resistant multi-wythe masonry walls should be designed assuming only the 
inner wythe acts as a structural wythe. Therefore, the inner wythe requires 
reinforcement, and the outer wythe may be unreinforced and acts only as additional 
mass. The ties connecting the wythes can be designed for conventional loads for this 
case, assuming the ties may fail during blast loading. However, failure of the outer 
wythe is a concern when the failed debris can fall onto an inhabited area or low roof 
area below. For cases where multi-wythe walls must stay attached, the ties should be 
designed in tension and compression for a pressure load equal to the smaller of; 1) the 
ultimate resistance of the outer wythe in flexure, 2) the ultimate resistance of the inner 
wythe in flexure, or 3) twice the peak blast pressure, acting over the tributary area 
supported by the tie with a safety factor of 1.3. The effects of any eccentricity in the

6-7.4.2 Connections.
Blast resistant masonry walls must have a connection at each support consisting of a 
connection plate bearing against the supporting component, embedded steel 
reinforcement (i.e. steel dowels into the foundation), or other form of positive connection 
that will resist the design reaction load from inbound and rebound response of the wall 
as determined by dynamic analyses (see Section 6-7.6). Usually a bearing connection 
is only effective for response in one direction (i.e. inbound wall response). Figure 6-4a
(+) and Figure 6-4b (+) show typical connections between non-load bearing masonry 
walls and supporting frame members in conventional construction. These connections 
are not recommended. They have relatively low strengths and typically, will not be 
sufficient to resist the design reaction loads. Connections for blast-loaded walls are 
often steel angles or plates that are bolted into the wall and supporting frame or 
foundation or welded to embedded structural steel plates. Figure 6-5 (+) shows generic 
blast-resistant connections.
6-7.4.3 Foundation Considerations.
The foundation system, such as that shown in Figure 6-6 (+), provides support at the 
bottom of masonry walls. Typically, the bottom of a masonry wall is connected with 
reinforcing steel dowels to a foundation consisting of a continuous reinforced concrete 
or CMU stem wall and spread footing. Conservatively, this connection can be assumed 
as a pinned connection when analyzing the flexural response of the wall, which limits 
the wall resistance to blast load, the reaction force at the foundation, and the 
corresponding shear stress in the wall. To help ensure that the wall responds 
consistently with this assumption, the splice between the dowels and vertical reinforcing 
steel should be limited to the compression splice length as would be typical for 
conventional design. Alternatively, for CMU walls designed to withstand an external 
blast load only, a full tension splice length located out of the maximum moment region 
can be provided and the wall’s support at the foundation may be analyzed as fixed if it 
can be shown that the ultimate soil pressures acting on the foundation can provide a 
resisting moment equal the ultimate dynamic moment capacity of the wall cross section 
at the base of the wall.

6-7.4.4 Framing and Shear Wall Considerations.
Masonry walls can function in the overall building system as load-bearing components 
or as non-load bearing components. In the latter case, the walls can be continuous in 
front of the supporting frame members or infill walls placed in the same plane as the 
frame between supporting frame members. The supporting frame can be a flexible type 
framing, such as structural steel framing, or more rigid framing, such as reinforced 
concrete frames. Figure 6-9 (+) illustrates these masonry support systems. Frame 
rigidity is important for infill masonry walls since it influences the amount of compression 
membrane response that can occur in masonry walls, as discussed in Section 6-7.5. 
Masonry walls can also act within a building system as shear walls. If the shear wall is 
exterior, it will nearly simultaneously resist directly applied blast loads and shear loads 
from the roof and floor diaphragms. In this case, the wall should have sufficient shear 
capacity to resist the combined out-of-plane shear force and in-plane shear force from 
the floor and roof diaphragms caused by blast loading as discussed in Section 6-7.3.4.
6-7.5 Compression and Tension Membrane Capacity.
Compression membrane response can only develop if there is a tight fit or very small 
gap (i.e. less than approximately 0.25 inch) between an infill masonry wall and 
surrounding rigid frame (e.g. infill walls surrounded by a heavy reinforced concrete 
frame). The amount of gap for infill walls can vary considerably depending on the 
construction. The walls can be built “tight” within the surrounding frame or there can be 
an intentional gap allowing the beam overhead to deflect without bearing on the wall 
below and for thermal expansion of the wall. Shrinkage of the wall may also increase 
the gap between the infill wall and surrounding frame. The amount of compression 
membrane response is very sensitive to the gap dimension and to the quality of the 
mortar between the masonry units and framing component. The use of compression 
membrane is not recommended for design due to general uncertainties in the actual 
gap dimension between the wall and surrounding building framing, the rigidity of the 
frame, and in quality of the mortar adjacent to the framing.

Tension membrane response can develop in reinforced masonry walls at large 
deflections if there is: 1) fully developed reinforcing bars throughout the span and into 
the supports; and 2) supports with adequate in-plane rigidity for one-way responding 
walls. The bars will not remain fully developed throughout the span if composite action 
between the grout and reinforcing steel deteriorates at high steel strains. Sufficient 
testing has not been performed to develop specific design and detailing requirements 
for masonry walls to provide tension membrane resistance during response to blast 
loads. Also, boundary conditions that allow tension membrane response are not typical 
in reinforced masonry wall construction. Thus, membrane action may develop in 
reinforced masonry walls with adequate supports and reinforcing details, but design 
approaches verified with blast test data are not available. 
In summary, tension and compression membrane action should not typically be 
considered in the design or analysis of masonry walls for explosives safety applications.
6-7.6 Dynamic Analysis.
The principles for dynamic analysis of structural components subject to blast loads 
presented in Chapter 3 of this document apply to blast analyses of masonry walls.
Therefore, the dynamic response of masonry walls can be modeled using an equivalent 
SDOF system. The SDOF system consists of a mass-spring system with a mass that is 
defined by the wall mass and a load-mass factor, and a spring that is defined by a 
resistance-deflection relationship representing the response of the wall to the blast load 
(i.e. typically a uniform pressure loading). The wall mass is calculated based on the wall 
weight and the load-mass factor is determined based on the wall boundary conditions 
and associated deflected shape of the wall from blast load as discussed in Section 3-7. 
The resistance-deflection relationship for flexural response is described in Chapter 3. 
This approach should be used to determine the resistance-deflection relationship for 
masonry walls. The ultimate dynamic moment capacity and moment of inertia for the 
wall should be determined as discussed in Sections 6.8 and 6.9 for reinforced and 
unreinforced masonry walls, respectively. At deflections greater than the yield 
deflection causing the ultimate flexural resistance, the response of reinforced masonry 
walls may be considered ductile (i.e. constant resistance equal to the ultimate flexural 
resistance with increasing deflection), while the response of unreinforced masonry walls 
is considered brittle and the flexural resistance goes to zero. Unreinforced masonry 
walls develop additional resistance at deflections greater than the yield deflection from 
the effects of in-plane axial force, including self-weight, which cause arching response 
described in Section 6-9. Given the one-story construction limitation in Section 6-7.1, 
the axial compression load on a masonry wall typically is low enough compared to the 
wall’s axial load capacity that the wall’s flexural design may ignore combined axial and 
flexure loads. 
The ultimate support shear, Vu, should be calculated at each support for the wall as 
discussed in Section 3-15. The wall’s connections to the supporting frame should be 
designed to resist the ultimate shear at the support and the wall’s shear capacity should

6-7.8 Rebound.
After reaching its initial maximum dynamic deflection, a masonry wall typically will 
deflect back in the opposite direction of the applied blast load (i.e. rebound). This 
causes a stress reversal in the wall and reaction forces that are in the opposite direction 
compared to the inbound response. Reinforcing steel located at the center of a masonry 
wall will resist both inbound and rebound stresses. Otherwise, new masonry walls 
should be symmetrically reinforced at each face. Unreinforced masonry walls should be 
analyzed assuming they have no flexural resistance and only axial load arching during 
rebound unless the inbound displacement was less than the flexural yield deflection, Xe
(see Section 6-9.2 and Figure 6-13 (+)). The response limits in Section 6-7.7 also apply 
during rebound. Masonry wall connections to their supports should resist the ultimate 
support shear for rebound response.

The amount of composite action between the reinforcing steel and masonry is very 
dependent on the workmanship during wall construction including the grout placement, 
since the grout transfers the tension strength of the reinforcing steel into the wall. This 
has been demonstrated in blast test programs on reinforced masonry walls, where walls 
with careful inspection during construction demonstrated significantly greater blast 
resistance than walls with no inspection (Oswald, et al 2006). Full composite action is 
necessary for reinforced masonry to develop a ductile resisting moment based on the 
dynamic tensile yield strength of reinforcement and the compression strength of the 
masonry, as assumed for blast design.
All blast resistant reinforced masonry walls should be subject to inspection and material 
testing during construction as required in Chapter 1 of TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 for 
essential facilities (i.e. Level C Quality Assurance). This requirement helps ensure that 
the reinforcing steel and surrounding grout will be constructed to act compositely with 
the surrounding masonry before and after yielding of the steel and that there are no 
voids in solid grouted walls that are intended to provide protection from fragments. 
Procedures that inhibit full composite action include insufficient vibrating during 
placement, improper reinforcing steel placement too near an edge of the grouted space, 
mortar protrusion and droppings into the grouted space, and grout with insufficient 
strength or plasticity. Additionally, both vertical and horizontal reinforcement should be 
accurately positioned during wall construction and rigidly secured at intervals by wire 
ties or spacing devices. The distance between reinforcement and the masonry unit 
must not be less than ¼-inch for fine grout or ½-inch for course grout.

6-8.3 Reinforcing Steel Limitations.
The maximum bar size for primary vertical reinforcing steel in new construction is a No. 
6 bar. There is also a limit of one bar per cell at each face of the masonry (i.e. no 
bundled bars). The purpose for these limitations is to encourage the use of more 
distributed reinforcement, rather than the use of a smaller number of larger bars. This 
requirement also helps to limit the demand on the grout to provide composite action 
between the reinforcement and masonry through large plastic deflections. Dynamic 
flexural response may be considered for existing walls with larger bar sizes if it can be 
shown that the masonry and grout can develop the full dynamic yield strength of the 
bars out to the maximum wall deflection calculated in the dynamic analysis of the wall 
response to the applied blast load.
Minimum and maximum values for the amount of steel reinforcement for blast design 
are shown in Table 6-4 (+). The minimum steel ratio for primary steel in the table is 
based on the requirement in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 for beams that the minimum 
steel ratio should cause a moment capacity that exceeds 1.3 times the masonry 
cracking moment, assuming that the dynamic modulus of rupture for masonry equals 
200 psi and the dynamic reinforcing steel yield strength is 77 ksi. The maximum steel 
ratio is based on a conservative, historical value from the Unified Building Code of one￾half the balanced steel ratio, assuming a dynamic prism strength of 2380 psi and a 
dynamic yield strength of 77 ksi for the reinforcing steel. This is less than the maximum 
steel ratio in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5, but blast testing shows that more ductility is 
available in reinforced masonry walls with lower steel ratios (Oswald et al, 2006). The 
minimum horizontal joint reinforcement is required to provide confinement around 
splices and distribute loads into the vertical reinforcing steel. Horizontal reinforcement 
should also be used as required around openings to distribute blast loads into primary 
vertical flexural steel and to resist conventional loads (e.g. in lintel beams over 
openings). 
The maximum spacing for primary vertical reinforcement is 3 times the nominal wall 
thickness for partially grouted walls and 6 times the nominal wall thickness for solid or 
fully grouted walls. The minimum spacing for primary vertical reinforcement in CMU 
walls is one bar per cell (or one bar at each face within a cell for nominal 12 inch thick 
CMU walls). Primary vertical reinforcement placed in voids between composite wythes 
of walls shall have a minimum spacing of 1.5 times the bar diameter, and not less than 
1.5 inches. The maximum and minimum spacing for secondary horizontal joint 
reinforcement is 16 inches and 8 inches, respectively.

6-9.2 Axial Load Arching.
For the general case where there is no compression membrane response, the 
resistance of an unreinforced masonry wall will reduce suddenly immediately after the 
wall resistance reaches the ultimate flexural resistance due to the brittle nature of the 
tensile bond between the mortar and masonry in the maximum moment regions. The 
wall will then develop axial load arching, which is characterized by a much lower 
resistance than flexural response, and a much larger deflection to failure. This provides 
a significant increase in the strain energy and blast load capacity of unreinforced 
masonry walls, although the wall is still a weak component against blast load. An 
example of the resistance-deflection curve for an unreinforced masonry wall with brittle 
flexural response followed by axial load arching is shown in Figure 6-11 (+). Note that 
the axial load is an applied load, and is therefore different from compression membrane 
response that depends on the in-plane rigidity of the supports to develop axial load. The 
applied axial load should only include the supported dead load. 
Figure 6-12 (+) illustrates the mechanics of axial load arching. As significant rotation 
occurs at midspan and at the supports after brittle flexural failure, the axial load is 
resisted at the unloaded face or the wall near the supports and at the loaded face near 
midspan. The resulting in–plane axial force couple (i.e. axial load arching) resists lateral 
load. The peak magnitude of the resisting couple, equal to Ra in Equation 6-13, is a 
function of the applied axial load, including component self-weight above midspan, and 
the component thickness. Note that the axial load from self weight in Equation 6-13 is 
assumed to act along the same line of action as the axial load, P, in Figure 6-12 when, 
in fact, it acts along a line of action through the center of gravity of the wall. This is a 
simplification that is supported by comparisons of SDOF analysis using Equation 6-13
to measured response of unreinforced masonry walls with axial load from self-weight 
(Oswald, 2005). The ultimate resistance and stiffness for flexural response used to 
calculate Xe in Equation 6-13 are calculated using methods in Chapter 3. Non-load 
bearing walls have axial load arching due only to axial load from their self-weight.

6-9.3 Unreinforced Multi-Wythe Walls.
Multi-wythe walls are generally designed to deflect together in a non-composite manner 
for conventional loads using ties to transfer load from the outer wythe to the inner 
wythe, as discussed in Section 6-7.3. For existing unreinforced masonry multi-wythe 
walls where it can be shown that the tie anchors will allow the outer wall to transfer load 
to the inner wall without failing according to the requirements in Section 6-7.3, the wall 
system can be analyzed as multiple walls responding together in flexure in a non￾composite manner analogous to a system of springs in parallel arrangement responding 
to a load. All the walls deflect equally, causing the system of walls to have a total 
flexural stiffness equal to the summed stiffnesses of all walls and to resist the applied 
blast load in proportion to their relative flexural stiffnesses. This is consistent with the 
approach recommended in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5. 
The walls are assumed to resist lateral load in this manner until the wall with the highest 
stresses yields in tension at all maximum moment locations to become a mechanism. 
The combined flexural resistance of all the walls when this occurs is the ultimate brittle 
flexural resistance of the multi-wythe wall system. Each wall must resist its ultimate 
support shear based on its maximum resistance.
After peak flexural response, the multi-wythe wall system can have axial load arching. 
The peak resistance from axial load arching is calculated separately for each wall and 
summed to determine the total peak axial load arching resistance for the wall system 
(i.e. Ra in Figure 6-13 (+)). The axial load arching resistance is assumed to decrease 
linearly to zero resistance at a deflection equal to the average wythe thickness, or to the 
thickness of the wythe providing the largest axial load arching resistance, whichever is 
less.
