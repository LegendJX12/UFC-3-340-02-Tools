# CHAPTER 6 SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN EXPLOSIVE FACILITY DESIGN 

## INTRODUCTION 

## 6 -1PURPOSE

The purpose of this manual is to present methods of design for protective construction used in facilities for development, testing, production, storage, maintenance,modification, inspection, demilitarization, and disposal of explosive materials.

## 6 -2OBJECTIVE

The primary objectives are to establish design procedures and construction techniques whereby propagation of explosion (from one structure or part of a structure to another)or mass detonation can be prevented and to provide protection for personnel and valuable equipment.



The secondary objectives are to:

(1)Establish the blast load parameters required for design of protective structures.



(2)Provide methods for calculating the dynamic response of structural elements including reinforced concrete, and structural steel.



(3)Establish construction details and procedures necessary to afford the required strength to resist the applied blast loads.

(4)Establish guidelines for siting explosive facilities to obtain maximum cost effectiveness in both the planning and structural arrangements, providing closures, and preventing damage to interior portions of structures because of structural motion, shock, and fragment perforation.



## 6 -3BACKGROUND

For the first 60 years of the 20th century, criteria and methods based upon results of catastrophic events were used for the design of explosive facilities. The criteria and methods did not include a detailed or reliable quantitative basis for assessing the degree of protection afforded by the protective facility. In the late 1960's quantitative procedures were set forth in the first edition of the present manual, "Structures to Resist the Effects of Accidental Explosions." This manual was based on extensive research and development programs which permitted a more reliable approach to current and future design requirements. Since the original publication of this manual, more extensive testing and development programs have taken place. This additional research included work with materials other than reinforced concrete which was the principal construction material referenced in the initial version of the manual.

<!-- batch 31 | page 13 -->

Modern methods for the manufacture and storage of explosive materials, which include many exotic chemicals, fuels, and propellants, require less space for a given quantity of explosive material than was previously needed. Such concentration of explosives increases the possibility of the propagation of accidental explosions (one accidental explosion causing the detonation of other explosive materials). It is evident that a requirement for more accurate design techniques is essential. This manual describes rational design methods to provide the required structural protection.

These design methods account for the close-in effects of a detonation including the high pressures and the nonuniformity of blast loading on protective structures or barriers.These methods also account for intermediate and far-range effects for the design of structures located away from the explosion. The dynamic response of structures,constructed of various materials, or combination of materials, can be calculated, and details are given to provide the strength and ductility required by the design. The design approach is directed primarily toward protective structures subjected to the effects of a high explosive detonation. However, this approach is general, and it is applicable to the design of other explosive environments as well as other explosive materials as mentioned above.



The design techniques set forth in this manual are based upon the results of numerous full- and small-scale structural response and explosive effects tests of various materials conducted in conjunction with the development of this manual and/or related projects.

## 6 -4SCOPE

It is not the intent of this manual to establish safety criteria. Applicable documents should be consulted for this purpose. Response predictions for personnel and equipment are included for information.



In this manual an effort is made to cover the more probable design situations. However,sufficient general information on protective design techniques has been included in order that application of the basic theory can be made to situations other than those which were fully considered.



This manual is applicable to the design of protective structures subjected to the effects associated with high explosive detonations. For these design situations, the manual wil apply for explosive quantities less than 25,000 pounds for close-in effects. However,this manual is also applicable to other situations such as far- or intermediate-range effects. For these latter cases the design procedures were originally developed for explosive quantities in the order of 500,000 pounds which is the maximum quantity of high explosive approved for aboveground storage facilities in the Department of Defense manual, "DoD Ammunition and Explosives Safety Standards,," DOD 6055.09M. \1\ However, DOD 6055.09-M currently addresses explosive quantities of up to 5,000,000 lbs, and the design procedures in this document have been extrapolated for designs to resist accidental explosions on ships of 5,000,000 lbs or more. Although some disasters have involved very high explosive quantities (such as Port Chicago in 1944 which involved around 10,000,000 pounds of explosives), limited testing has been 

<!-- batch 31 | page 14 -->

completed at such high quantities (e.g. the Middle North Series used 1,000,00 Ibs TNT, and the Minor Scale and Misty Picture used the equivalent of 8,000,000 Ibs TNT)./1/ Since tests were primarily directed toward the response of structural steel and reinforced concrete elements to blast overpressures, this manual concentrates on design procedures and techniques for these materials. However, this does not imply that concrete and steel are the only useful materials for protective construction. Tests to establish the response of wood, brick blocks, and plastics, as well as the blast attenuating and mass effects of soil are contemplated. The results of these tests may require, at a later date, the supplementation of these design methods for these and other materials.



Other manuals are available to design protective structures against the effects of high explosive or nuclear detonations. The procedures in these manuals will quite often complement this manual and should be consulted for specific applications.

## 6 -6CHAPTER CONTENTS

This chapter contains procedures for the design of blast resistant structures other than above ground, cast-in-place concrete or structural steel structures, as well as the design of other miscellaneous blast resistant components. Included herein is the design of reinforced and non-reinforced masonry walls, precast elements with both prestressed and conventional reinforcement, pre-engineered buildings, suppressive shielding, blast resistant windows, underground structures, earth-covered arch-type magazines, blast valves and shock isolation systems.

## MASONRY 

## 6 -7

## GENERAL INFORMATION

Masonry walls can provide economical resistance to relatively low external blast overpressures when properly designed and detailed, along with other advantageous characteristics such as limited fragment protection (solid masonry), fire protection,acoustical and thermal insulation, and modular construction. General requirements and limitations on the use of masonry for blast resistant design are discussed in this section.Sections 6-8 and 6-9 discuss specific requirements related to blast design with reinforced masonry and unreinforced masonry, respectively.



The primary application of masonry units in blast resistant design is wall construction Basic variations in wall configurations include the type of masonry unit, such as brick,

<!-- batch 31 | page 16 -->

clay tile or CMU (concrete masonry unit); the manner in which these units are laid (running bond, stack bond, etc.); the amount of void space in the masonry; the number of wythes of masonry units through the wall thickness (i.e. single or double); and, the amount of steel reinforcement (reinforced or unreinforced masonry). In all cases masonry is similar to concrete in that it is relatively strong in compression and much weaker in tension. Therefore, structural masonry walls are commonly reinforced with vertical reinforcing steel bars that are the same as those used in concrete and with secondary steel wire reinforcement (i.e. joint reinforcement) that is placed horizontally in the bed joints between masonry units.



### 6 -7.1Limits on the Use of Masonry Construction

New masonry construction in this UFC is limited to one-story, reinforced CMU walls placed in a running bond with single or multiple wythes. CMU walls may be designed to satisfy Protection Category 1 and Protection Category 2 requirements. All new CMU walls shall be reinforced with vertical steel reinforcing bars that are designed in accordance with Section 6.8. The information in Section 6.9 can be used to determine if existing unreinforced masonry walls provide a sufficient level of protection.

Non-solid masonry walls with any ungrouted voids, including reinforced masonry walls that are not fully grouted, may only be used to provide protection against far-range,externalblast loading(i.e.onlyatchargescaledstandoffsgreaterthan3ft/lb/)where there is no direct primary or secondary fragmentation threat. This requirement effectively limits use of these walls to building sides that do not face towards the potential explosion site. Solid, or fully grouted reinforced masonry walls can be designed to provide required fragment protection as described in Section 6-7.3.7. They may also be used at close-in scaled standoffs (i.e. at charge scaled standoff distances of 3 ft/lb 1/3and less) if they can be designed to satisfy the required Protection Category requirements and spall protection as described in Section 6-7.3.6.

The design of new masonry walls to resist internal blast loads is not recommended as there areverylimited dataforthisapplication. For applications involving existing masonry, the wall may not be subjected to combined flexure and tension under internal or external blast loading. Masonry walls may not be used as substantial dividing walls.Masonry walls are designed to resist blast loads in flexure. Per Section 6-7.4, tension membrane action and compression membrane action may not be considered. For existing unreinforced masonry walls, the additional resistance from axial load arching may be incorporated into the resistance-deflection curve in accordance with Section 69.2.



### 6 -7.2Materials

Masonry materials consist of masonry units and mortar, which is used to connect the units into a structural component. They can also include reinforcing steel and grout,which causes the reinforcing steel to be composite with the masonry, as well as secondary wire reinforcement placed in the horizontal bed joints between courses of 

<!-- batch 31 | page 17 -->

masonry units. Only vertical steel reinforcement that is grouted into the masonry cells should be used as flexural reinforcement for blast resistant design. Wire reinforcement may be used in horizontal bed joints as required for temperature reinforcement, but should not be considered flexural reinforcement. Masonry walls can have multiple wythes of masonry that are connected with ties placed in the mortar beds of each wythe.



#### 6 -7.2.1Masonry, Mortar, and Grout Materials

Figure 6-1 (+) shows typical shapes for different types of masonry units. Brick and CMU are the most commonly used masonry units in the United States (U.S.) and Canada,with some use of clay tile. Generally, a masonry unit is described based on its type and its nominal thickness dimension. The actual dimensions of manufactured masonry units are 3/8 inch less than nominal dimensions to allow for the mortar thickness and the thickness of typical interior wall material (i.e. gypsum board). All masonry units must conform to applicable ASTM requirements in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 (e.g. CMU must conform to the requirements of ASTM C90). ###### 6 -7.4Masonry Wall Construction.6-7.4.1Multi-Wythe Walls

A masonry wall can be constructed as a single, monolithic wythe with one or more units through the thickness acting compositely, or as a cavity wall consisting of multiple,closely spaced masonry wythes with an air gap or insulation between wythes. Many modern masonry walls in the northern part of the U.S. and Canada are cavity walls to provide required insulation and help prevent moisture migration into the building. Figure 6-3 (+) shows a cavity wall constructed with brick and CMU. Cavity walls can also be constructed with other types of masonry. These walls are connected together across the gap between wythes with ties or tabs, as illustrated in Figure 6-3, which cause both wythes to deflect together in a non-composite manner when conventional lateral loads are applied to the outer wythe. The outer wythe of multi-wythe walls may be referred to as a veneer wall, which is typically an unreinforced, non-structural wall that acts only as a facade and prevents moisture penetration.



New blast-resistant multi-wythe masonry walls should be designed assuming only the inner wythe acts as a structural wythe. Therefore, the inner wythe requires reinforcement, and the outer wythe may be unreinforced and acts only as additional mass. The ties connecting the wythes can be designed for conventional loads for this case, assuming the ties may fail during blast loading. However, failure of the outer wythe is a concern when the failed debris can fall onto an inhabited area or low roof area below. For cases where multi-wythe walls must stay attached, the ties should be designed in tension and compression for a pressure load equal to the smaller of; 1) the ultimate resistance of the outer wythe in flexure, 2) the ultimate resistance of the inner wythe in flexure, or 3) twice the peak blast pressure, acting over the tributary area supported by the tie with a safety factor of 1.3. The effects of any eccentricity in the 

<!-- batch 31 | page 27 -->

connection points of the ties into each wythe on the connector capacity should be considered.



In conventional construction, multi-wythe walls with the same material in each wythe can be designed to act compositely, where the inner wall is in tension and the outer wal is in compression. However, this approach is impractical and is not recommended for blast design of reinforced masonry walls because of the large number of steel stirrups required between the wythes to transfer the full dynamic yield strength of the reinforcing steel into the adjacent wythe. Composite and non-composite unreinforced multi-wythe masonry walls are discussed more in Section 6.9.3.



#### 6 -7.4.2Connections

Blast resistant masonry walls must have a connection at each support consisting of a connection plate bearing against the supporting component, embedded steel reinforcement (i.e. steel dowels into the foundation), or other form of positive connection that will resist the design reaction load from inbound and rebound response of the wall as determined by dynamic analyses (see Section 6-7.6). Usually a bearing connection is only effective for response in one direction (i.e. inbound wall response). #### 6 -7.4.3Foundation Considerations

The foundation system, such as that shown in Figure 6-6 (+), provides support at the bottom of masonry walls. #### 6 -7.4.4Framing and Shear Wall Considerations

Masonry walls can function in the overall building system as load-bearing components or as non-load bearing components. In the latter case, the walls can be continuous in front of the supporting frame members or infill walls placed in the same plane as the frame between supporting frame members. The supporting frame can be a flexible type framing, such as structural steel framing, or more rigid framing, such as reinforced concrete frames. Figure 6-9 (+) illustrates these masonry support systems. Frame rigidity is important for infill masonry walls since it influences the amount of compression membrane response that can occur in masonry walls, as discussed in Section 6-7.5.

Masonry walls can also act within a building system as shear walls. If the shear wall is exterior, it will nearly simultaneously resist directly applied blast loads and shear loads from the roof and floor diaphragms. In this case, the wall should have sufficient shear capacity to resist the combined out-of-plane shear force and in-plane shear force from the floor and roof diaphragms caused by blast loading as discussed in Section 6-7.3.4.

### 6 -7.5Compression and Tension Membrane Capacity

Compression membrane response can only develop if there is a tight fit or very small gap (i.e. less than approximately 0.25 inch) between an infill masonry wall and surrounding rigid frame (e.g. infill walls surrounded by a heavy reinforced concrete frame). The amount of gap for infill walls can vary considerably depending on the construction. The walls can be built "tight" within the surrounding frame or there can be an intentional gap allowing the beam overhead to deflect without bearing on the wall below and for thermal expansion of the wall. Shrinkage of the wall may also increase the gap between the infill wall and surrounding frame. The amount of compression membrane response is very sensitive to the gap dimension and to the quality of the mortar between the masonry units and framing component. The use of compression membrane is not recommended for design due to general uncertainties in the actual gap dimension between the wall and surrounding building framing, the rigidity of the frame, and in quality of the mortar adjacent to the framing.

Tension membrane response can develop in reinforced masonry walls at large deflections if there is: 1) fully developed reinforcing bars throughout the span and into the supports; and 2) supports with adequate in-plane rigidity for one-way responding walls. The bars will not remain fully developed throughout the span if composite action between the grout and reinforcing steel deteriorates at high steel strains. Sufficient testing has not been performed to develop specific design and detailing requirements for masonry walls to provide tension membrane resistance during response to blast loads. Also, boundary conditions that allow tension membrane response are not typical in reinforced masonry wall construction. Thus, membrane action may develop in reinforced masonry walls with adequate supports and reinforcing details, but design approaches verified with blast test data are not available.



In summary, tension and compression membrane action should not typically be considered in the design or analysis of masonry walls for explosives safety applications.

### 6 -7.6Dynamic Analysis

The principles for dynamic analysis of structural components subject to blast loads presented in Chapter 3 of this document apply to blast analyses of masonry walls.Therefore, the dynamic response of masonry walls can be modeled using an equivalent SDOF system. The SDOF system consists of a mass-spring system with a mass that is defined by the wall mass and a load-mass factor, and a spring that is defined by a resistance-deflection relationship representing the response of the wall to the blast load (i.e. typically a uniform pressure loading). The wall mass is calculated based on the wall weight and the load-mass factor is determined based on the wall boundary conditions and associated deflected shape of the wall from blast load as discussed in Section 3-7.The resistance-deflection relationship for flexural response is described in Chapter 3.This approach should be used to determine the resistance-deflection relationship for masonry walls. The ultimate dynamic moment capacity and moment of inertia for the wall should be determined as discussed in Sections 6.8 and 6.9 for reinforced and unreinforced masonry walls, respectively. At deflections greater than the yield deflection causing the ultimate flexural resistance, the response of reinforced masonry walls may be considered ductile (i.e. constant resistance equal to the ultimate flexural resistance with increasing deflection), while the response of unreinforced masonry walls is considered britle and the flexural resistance goes to zero. Unreinforced masonry walls develop additional resistance at deflections greater than the yield deflection from the effects of in-plane axial force, including self-weight, which cause arching response described in Section 6-9. Given the one-story construction limitation in Section 6-7.1,the axial compression load on a masonry wall typically is low enough compared to the wall's axial load capacity that the wall's flexural design may ignore combined axial and flexure loads.



The ultimate support shear,$\mathsf{V}_{\mathsf{u}}$ , should be calculated at each support for the wall as discussed in Section 3-15. The wall's connections to the supporting frame should be designed to resist the ultimate shear at the support and the wall's shear capacity should

be designed to resist the ultimate shear at the support or at a distance d from the support as discussed in Section 6-7.3.4.

### 6 -7.8Rebound

After reaching its initial maximum dynamic deflection, a masonry wall typically will deflect back in the opposite direction of the applied blast load (i.e. rebound). This causes a stress reversal in the wall and reaction forces that are in the opposite direction compared to the inbound response. Reinforcing steel located at the center of a masonry wall will resist both inbound and rebound stresses. Otherwise, new masonry walls should be symmetrically reinforced at each face. Unreinforced masonry walls should be analyzed assuming they have no flexural resistance and only axial load arching during rebound unless the inbound displacement was less than the flexural yield deflection, Xe (see Section 6-9.2 and Figure 6-13 (+)). The response limits in Section 6-7.7 also apply during rebound. Masonry wall connections to their supports should resist the ultimate support shear for rebound response.

### 6 -8.1General Requirements and Construction Procedures

Reinforced masonry walls are designed to resist blast load in a similar manner as reinforced concrete walls, except that the walls must comply with construction requirements and reinforcement steel detailling provisions of TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5.

<!-- batch 31 | page 31 -->

The amount of composite action between the reinforcing steel and masonry is very dependent on the workmanship during wall construction including the grout placement,since the grout transfers the tension strength of the reinforcing steel into the wall. This has been demonstrated in blast test programs on reinforced masonry walls, where walls with careful inspection during construction demonstrated significantly greater blast resistance than walls with no inspection (Oswald, et al 2006). Full composite action is necessary for reinforced masonry to develop a ductile resisting moment based on the dynamic tensile yield strength of reinforcement and the compression strength of the masonry, as assumed for blast design.



All blast resistant reinforced masonry walls should be subject to inspection and material testing during construction as required in Chapter 1 of TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 for essential facilities (i.e. Level C Quality Assurance). This requirement helps ensure that the reinforcing steel and surrounding grout will be constructed to act compositely with the surrounding masonry before and after yielding of the steel and that there are no voids in solid grouted walls that are intended to provide protection from fragments.Procedures that inhibit full composite action include insufficient vibrating during placement, improper reinforcing steel placement too near an edge of the grouted space,mortar protrusion and droppings into the grouted space, and grout with insufficient strength or plasticity. Additionally, both vertical and horizontal reinforcement should be accurately positioned during wall construction and rigidly secured at intervals by wire ties or spacing devices. The distance between reinforcement and the masonry unit must not be less than -inch for fine grout or /-inch for course grout.

### 6 -8.3Reinforcing Steel Limitations

The maximum bar size for primary vertical reinforcing steel in new construction is a No.6 bar. There is also a limit of one bar per cell at each face of the masonry (i.e. no bundled bars). The purpose for these limitations is to encourage the use of more distributed reinforcement, rather than the use of a smaller number of larger bars. This requirement also helps to limit the demand on the grout to provide composite action between the reinforcement and masonry through large plastic deflections. Dynamic flexural response may be considered for existing walls with larger bar sizes if it can be shown that the masonry and grout can develop the full dynamic yield strength of the bars out to the maximum wall deflection calculated in the dynamic analysis of the wall response to the applied blast load.



Minimum and maximum values for the amount of steel reinforcement for blast design are shown in Table 6-4 (+). The minimum steel ratio for primary steel in the table is based on the requirement in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5 for beams that the minimum steel ratio should cause a moment capacity that exceeds 1.3 times the masonry cracking moment, assuming that the dynamic modulus of rupture for masonry equals 200 psi and the dynamic reinforcing steel yield strength is 77 ksi. The maximum steel ratio is based on a conservative, historical value from the Unified Building Code of onehalf the balanced steel ratio, assuming a dynamic prism strength of 2380 psi and a dynamic yield strength of 77 ksi for the reinforcing steel. This is less than the maximum steel ratio in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5, but blast testing shows that more ductility is available in reinforced masonry walls with lower steel ratios (Oswald et al, 2006). The minimum horizontal joint reinforcement is required to provide confinement around splices and distribute loads into the vertical reinforcing steel. Horizontal reinforcement should also be used as required around openings to distribute blast loads into primary vertical flexural steel and to resist conventional loads (e.g. in lintel beams over openings).

The minimum spacing for primary vertical reinforcement in CMU walls is one bar per cell (or one bar at each face within a cell for nominal 12 inch thick CMU walls). ### 6 -9.3Unreinforced Multi-Wythe Walls

Multi-wythe walls are generally designed to deflect together in a non-composite manner for conventional loads using ties to transfer load from the outer wythe to the inner wythe, as discussed in Section 6-7.3.For existing unreinforced masonry multi-wythe walls where it can be shown that the tie anchors will allow the outer wall to transfer load to the inner wall without failing according to the requirements in Section 6-7.3, the wall system can be analyzed as multiple walls responding together in flexure in a noncomposite manner analogous to a system of springs in parallel arrangement responding to a load. All the walls deflect equally, causing the system of walls to have a total flexural stiffness equal to the summed stiffnesses of all walls and to resist the applied blast load in proportion to their relative flexural stiffnesses. This is consistent with the approach recommended in TMS 402/ACI 530/ASCE 5.



The walls are assumed to resist lateral load in this manner until the wall with the highest stresses yields in tension at all maximum moment locations to become a mechanism.The combined flexural resistance of all the walls when this occurs is the ultimate brittle flexural resistance of the multi-wythe wall system. Each wall must resist its ultimate support shear based on its maximum resistance.



After peak flexural response, the multi-wythe wall system can have axial load arching.The peak resistance from axial load arching is calculated separately for each wall and summed to determine the total peak axial load arching resistance for the wall system (i.e.$\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{a}}$ in Figure 6-13 (+)).
